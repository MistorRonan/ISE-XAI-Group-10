In the report-vs-promote test (Exp~1), \texttt{original} still scored 7 of the 16 sentences that report abuse above 0.5 (mean toxicity 0.43, against 0.88 for promoting and 0.57 for counter-speech sentences), and SHAP placed 87\% of the toward-toxic attribution in report sentences on the insult words (75\% for promote, 69\% for counter).

In the identity-term swap (Exp~2), harmless sentences containing an identity term received a mean toxicity of 0.124 from \texttt{original} and 0.106 from \texttt{unbiased}, against 0.001 and 0.002 when the same templates contain a control word ($n = 150$ and 40). SHAP placed 46\% (\texttt{original}) and 60\% (\texttt{unbiased}) of its total absolute attribution on the identity word, against about 20\% on a control word, and SHAP and LIME chose the same top word in 78 of the 88 sentences explained by both.

Table~\ref{tab:faith} summarises every faithfulness result. In the counterfactual test (Exp~3), the true effect of identity term $t$ and SHAP's estimate of it are
\begin{equation}
\Delta_t = \ell(x_t) - \overline{\ell(x_c)}, \qquad
\hat{\Delta}_t = \phi_t - \overline{\phi_c},
\label{eq:cf}
\end{equation}
where $\ell$ is the filter's log-odds, $\phi$ is a SHAP value and the bars denote the mean over the four control-word versions $x_c$ of the same template. Fig.~\ref{fig:cf} plots $\hat{\Delta}_t$ against $\Delta_t$. SHAP ranks the effects almost perfectly ($\rho = 0.96$ for \texttt{original}, 0.92 for \texttt{unbiased}; 0.98 and 0.95 per term) and gives the correct sign in 98\% and 93\% of sentences. Its magnitudes are less accurate: the mean absolute error is 0.94 and 0.72 log-odds, and 83\% and 75\% of points lie above $y = x$, so SHAP tends to overstate the effect. LIME reaches $\rho = 0.97$ for both models.

These results use SHAP's default baseline, which replaces a removed word with ``...''. Deleting the word instead, or using the model's mask token, barely changes the ranking of identity terms ($\rho \geq 0.97$ between every pair of baselines) but does change the magnitudes: SHAP's error against the counterfactual effect falls from 0.94 (dots) to 0.84 (delete) to 0.70 log-odds (mask) for \texttt{original}, and from 0.72 to 0.58 to 0.44 for \texttt{unbiased}.

On the transparent filter (Exp~4), SHAP's Partition approximation matched the exact Shapley values almost perfectly (median $\rho = 0.993$, MAE 0.022 log-odds), dropping only slightly on high-interaction sentences ($\rho = 0.986$ against 1.000), and LIME's top word matched the exact one in 96\% of runs. The filter's own coefficients are positive for 14 of the 15 identity words (e.g.\ gay $+2.83$, muslim $+1.90$ log-odds) and negative for all four control words ($-0.26$ to $-0.39$).

\subsection{Deletion Test}\label{sec:res-del}
% Exp 5 (Bláithín). Sources: exp5_aopc.csv, exp5_aopc_by_group.csv.
% Cut for space: SHAP gap largest on Exp 1 counter / report sentences (4.58 /
% 4.52); control comprehensiveness SHAP -0.02, LIME 0.01; unbiased
% sufficiency SHAP -0.97, LIME -0.85, random +2.63. Negative sufficiency is

Deleting the top-ranked words lowered the toxic log-odds far more than deleting random words: comprehensiveness was 3.09 for SHAP and 3.05 for LIME against 0.35 for random words (\texttt{original}; 4.02, 4.06 and 0.95 for \texttt{unbiased}). The gap was largest on the Exp~1 counter and report sentences and close to zero on control sentences. Sufficiency was negative for both explainers ($-2.43$ and $-2.65$) but positive for random words (1.30), so the top-ranked words alone scored higher than the full sentence, most strongly in report sentences ($-3.4$).

The stability test (Exp~6) reruns LIME with 10 seeds at 500 and 5000 samples on all 238 sentences from Exps~1--2, explaining \texttt{original}. The top word changes between seeds in 12\% of sentences at 500 samples and in 4\% at 5000. Fig.~\ref{fig:stability} plots each sentence's instability (1 minus the mean Spearman $\rho$ between runs) against its signal strength $s = |\ell(x) - \phi_0|$, where $\phi_0$ is SHAP's base value: the two are unrelated ($|\rho| < 0.05$). Signal strength does, however, predict how well LIME's surrogate fits ($\rho = 0.73$ with $R^2$): at 5000 samples the mean $R^2$ is 0.41 for the weaker half of sentences against 0.77 for the stronger half, and LIME's top word matches SHAP's in 86\% against 95\%.

Finally, we tested whether an auditor, who cannot compute the counterfactual effect, could tell which SHAP explanations are wrong. We marked the 25\% of identity sentences with the largest error $|\Delta_t - \hat{\Delta}_t|$ and measured how well three cues that need no reference identify them (AUC: 0.5 is chance, 1 is perfect). Weak signal strength identified them best (AUC 0.80), followed by low LIME $R^2$ (0.64), while SHAP--LIME disagreement did no better than chance (0.46).
