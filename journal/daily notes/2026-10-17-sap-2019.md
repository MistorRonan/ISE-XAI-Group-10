## Source

M. Sap, D. Card, S. Gabriel, Y. Choi, and N. A. Smith, "The risk of racial bias in hate speech detection," in *Proc. 57th Annu. Meeting Assoc. Computational Linguistics (ACL)*, Florence, Italy, 2019, pp. 1668–1678, doi: 10.18653/v1/P19-1163.
PDF: https://aclanthology.org/P19-1163/

## TL;DR

The authors show that widely used hate-speech Twitter datasets have **racial bias built into their labels**: tweets in African American English (AAE) are much more likely to be annotated as offensive or abusive. Classifiers trained on these datasets learn and spread that bias. On other Twitter corpora, AAE tweets are **more than twice as likely** to be labelled "offensive"/"abusive", and tweets by self-identified African American users are **1.5 times** more likely to be labelled "offensive". They also show that telling annotators a tweet's likely dialect or the author's likely race ("priming") makes them significantly less likely to label AAE tweets as offensive. For us, it's the source behind the README's claim that "hate-speech detectors flag African American English as toxic", and evidence that **the same word can be harmless or offensive depending on who is speaking**.

## Key takeaways

- **Motivating example (Fig. 1):** PerspectiveAPI (Jigsaw/Alphabet's toxicity tool) scores AAE phrases far higher than their non-AAE equivalents. Pairs come from Spears (1998), and the authors say AAE speakers understand them as non-toxic:
  - "Wussup, n\*gga!" 90% vs "What's up, bro!" 7%.
  - "I saw his ass yesterday." 95% vs "I saw him yesterday." 6%.
- **Dialect as a proxy for race (§2):** the authors infer dialect with Blodgett et al.'s (2016) topic model (trained on 60M geolocated tweets, using US census race/ethnicity data), which gives the probability that a tweet is AAE (p_AAE) or White-aligned English. They stress AAE is "common among, but not unique to" African Americans.
- **Datasets (§3, Table 1):** both labelled by Figure-Eight crowdworkers.
  - **DWMW17** (Davidson et al., 2017): 24,783 tweets labelled hate speech / offensive / none. Collected starting from 1,000 HateBase terms, with at least three annotations per tweet.
  - **FDCL18** (Founta et al., 2018): 99,996 tweets labelled hateful / abusive / spam / none, five annotators each.
- **Data bias (§3.1, Table 1):** p_AAE correlates with the **"offensive" label in DWMW17 (r = 0.42)** and the **"abusive" label in FDCL18 (r = 0.35)**, and negatively with "none" (−0.414 and −0.307).
- **Bias in trained models (§3.2, Fig. 2):** a BiLSTM-with-attention classifier initialised with GloVe vectors, trained on each dataset. Footnote 7: in preliminary experiments the findings held regardless of the classifier.
  - **False positives on test data:**
    - DWMW17 model: **46.3%** of non-offensive AAE tweets were labelled "offensive", vs **9.0%** for White-aligned tweets.
    - FDCL18 model: "abusive" false positives were 26.0% for AAE vs 4.5% for White-aligned.
    - Both models tended to label White-aligned tweets "none" (false "none": 7.9% vs 1.1% on DWMW17, 30.5% vs 4.2% on FDCL18).
    - The authors say these gaps violate **equality of opportunity** (Hardt et al., 2016).
  - **On other Twitter corpora:**
    - **DEMOGRAPHIC16** (56M tweets, dialect labels assigned above 80% probability): the share of tweets labelled toxic was 38.7% for AAE vs 18.5% for White-aligned English with the DWMW17 model ("offensive"), and 24.6% vs 11.4% with the FDCL18 model ("abusive").
    - **USERLEVELRACE18** (5.4M tweets from 4,132 survey participants who self-reported race: 3,184 White, 374 African American): "offensive" was 20.0% for African American authors vs 13.5% for White authors.
- **Annotator priming study (§4, Fig. 3):** Amazon Mechanical Turk, 1,351 tweets stratified by dialect, toxicity category and dataset, with five annotations each and a separate set of workers for each condition. *Dialect priming* shows the tweet's inferred dialect; *race priming* shows the author's likely race.
  - "Offensive to anyone" (no = 0, maybe = 0.5, yes = 1): the control mean was **0.55**, vs **0.44** for both dialect and race priming (p ≪ 0.001).
  - "Offensive to you": only race priming differed significantly (0.33 → 0.25, p ≪ 0.001).
  - Annotators were more likely to rate a tweet offensive to *someone* than to *themselves*. The authors suggest this means people recognise that offensiveness is subjective.
- **Which words drive it (Appendix A.1, Fig. 4):** in unigram logistic regression models:
  - The most highly weighted words for "hateful" in FDCL18 are "n\*gga" and "n\*ggas", which are strongly associated with AAE.
  - In DWMW17, the HateBase seed list leaves artefacts: "charlie", "bird" and "yankees" get negative weights.
  - Leaving out one word at a time, almost all correlations hold. The exception: without tweets containing "n\*gga"/"n\*ggas", the FDCL18 "hateful" correlation drops to r = 0.047 (while "abusive" rises to r = 0.376).
- **PerspectiveAPI (Appendix A.4):** TOXICITY scores (API accessed December 2018) correlate positively with AAE in all four datasets: r = 0.310 (DWMW17), 0.453 (FDCL18), 0.056 (DEMOGRAPHIC16), 0.042 (USERLEVELRACE18, self-reported African American authors). All are significant after Holm correction.
- **Main argument:** "text alone does not determine offensiveness". Toxicity depends on social context, such as the speaker's identity and dialect. Earlier bias work focused on explicit identity mentions (e.g. Dixon et al., 2018). This paper shows **bias against minorities can occur even without any identity mention**, through dialect. Extra attention should be paid to the confounding effects of dialect.

## Why it matters for our project

1. **It's motivating evidence for our topic.** It backs the README's claim that "hate-speech detectors flag African American English as toxic". In a removal setting like pretraining-data filtering, those flags would mean AAE text gets dropped (the paper's introduction warns that automated removal "risks further suppressing already-marginalized voices"). Pairs naturally with Dodge et al. (2021) on C4's blocklist removing AAE text.
2. **It supports Experiment 1's question.** Fig. 1 and Appendix A.1 show classifiers reacting to individual words ("ass", "n\*gga") regardless of who is speaking or what they mean. That is the "does the filter react to the word or the intent?" question behind Experiment 1.
3. **It shows a gap in Experiment 2's scope.** Experiment 2 swaps explicit identity words, which is Dixon et al.'s approach. This paper argues that kind of test misses bias carried by **dialect without any identity mention**. We should state this as a limitation of Experiment 2. *(The link to our experiment is our reasoning; the paper only makes the general point.)*
4. **A word-level explanation was how they found the cause.** Appendix A.1 uses the weights of a simple linear model to find which words drive the labels, and shows how a dataset's construction (the HateBase seed list) leaves visible artefacts. That's an early example of using word-level attribution to audit a toxicity dataset, the same kind of reasoning we apply to SHAP/LIME outputs on Detoxify.
5. **It warns that labels themselves can be biased.** The bias starts with annotators, not the model. If Detoxify's training labels have the same problem, SHAP/LIME will faithfully explain a biased model; they can't tell us the labels were wrong. That fits our README's point that attributions are descriptive, not normative.

## Concerns / why we should be careful with it

- **Dialect is a proxy for race:** the authors say so themselves. The dialect detector is lexical, so some words (e.g. "n\*gga") count both as AAE markers and as toxic features. Their leave-one-word-out check found the correlations mostly robust, but the FDCL18 "hateful" correlation mostly disappeared once those words were removed.
- **It rests on an assumption:** footnote 5 assumes "a priori that the average tweet is not inherently more toxic in a particular dialect". The authors note that checking this "requires a deep understanding of sociocultural norms".
- **Old models and different data:** a 2019 BiLSTM + GloVe on tweets, and PerspectiveAPI as accessed in December 2018. **Detoxify isn't tested**, and tweets aren't web pretraining documents. The kind of bias transfers as a hypothesis; the numbers don't.
- **The priming study is small and its workers skewed:** about 75% of workers self-reported as White (110, 143 and 81 workers per condition). The authors note that wanting not to seem prejudiced could influence answers. The priming instructions also told workers directly that words like "n\*gga" or the suffix "-ass" are usually harmless when used by Black speakers (Fig. 6), so the effect is partly explicit instruction, not just dialect awareness.
- **Priming results are mixed:** for "offensive to you", only race priming was significant; dialect priming wasn't.
- **WH16 results are weak:** the extra analysis on Waseem and Hovy (2016) is limited by severe sampling bias (70% of sexist tweets from two users, 99% of racist tweets from one user). The authors say to interpret it cautiously.
- **Don't confuse it with Sap et al. (2021/2022):** other journal entries list "Sap et al. (2021) on annotator bias in toxicity detection" as a follow-up. That's a different paper from this 2019 ACL one.

## Where this ties back into XAI module content

- **Stakeholders / the Auditor (week 1, W1-L01 p5):** the paper is a bias audit of toxicity datasets and classifiers: do they treat AAE and White-aligned English equally?
- **Global vs local explanations (week 4, W4-L01 p6):** Appendix A.1's unigram weights are a global, model-wide view of which words matter. Our SHAP/LIME work gives local, per-sentence explanations that we then aggregate.
- **Don't infer causality from attributions (Textbook p53):** the authors say their word-level analysis "does not completely explain the correlations". High feature weights show association with a label, not that a word causes the offence.
- **Fairness criteria:** the paper uses equality of opportunity (Hardt et al., 2016), i.e. equal false positive rates across groups, as its fairness test. A useful contrast with attribution-based audits.
