In the identity-term swap (Exp~2), every sentence is harmless, yet those containing an identity term receive a mean toxicity probability of 0.124 from \texttt{original} and 0.106 from \texttt{unbiased}, compared with 0.001 and 0.002 when the same templates contain a control word ($n = 150$ and 40 sentences). Both explainers attribute this difference to the identity word. SHAP assigns it 46\% (\texttt{original}) and 60\% (\texttt{unbiased}) of the total absolute attribution, against about 20\% for a control word, and SHAP and LIME select the same top word in 78 of the 88 sentences explained by both (43/48 in Exp~1, 35/40 in Exp~2). Agreement of this kind does not show that either explanation is correct~\cite{krishna2022disagreement}.

Table~\ref{tab:faith} summarises every faithfulness result. In the counterfactual test (Exp~3), the true effect of identity term $t$ and SHAP's estimate of it are:
\begin{equation}
\Delta_t = \ell(x_t) - \overline{\ell(x_c)}, \qquad
\hat{\Delta}_t = \phi_t - \overline{\phi_c},
\label{eq:cf}
\end{equation}
where $\ell$ is the filter's log-odds, $\phi$ is a SHAP value and the bars denote the mean over the four control-word versions $x_c$ of the same template. Fig.~\ref{fig:cf} plots $\hat{\Delta}_t$ against $\Delta_t$. SHAP ranks the effects almost perfectly ($\rho = 0.96$ for \texttt{original}, 0.92 for \texttt{unbiased}; 0.98 and 0.95 per term) and gives the correct sign in 98\% and 93\% of sentences. Its magnitudes are less accurate: the mean absolute error is 0.94 and 0.72 log-odds, and 83\% and 75\% of points lie above $y = x$, so SHAP tends to overstate the effect. LIME reaches $\rho = 0.97$ for both models.

The stability test (Exp~6) reruns LIME with 10 seeds at 500 and 5000 samples on all 238 sentences from Exp~1/2, explaining \texttt{original}. With 500 samples the top word changes between seeds in 12\% of sentences,with 5000 samples it changes in 4\%. Fig.~\ref{fig:stability} plots each sentence's instability, $1 - \bar{\rho}$ (where $\bar{\rho}$ is the mean Spearman correlation between the word rankings of every pair of runs), against its signal strength $s = |\ell(x) - \phi_0|$, the distance between its log-odds and SHAP's base value $\phi_0$. The two are unrelated ($|\rho| < 0.05$). However, signal strength does predict how well LIME's surrogate fits ($\rho = 0.73$ with $R^2$): at 5000 samples the mean $R^2$ is 0.41 for the weaker half of sentences against 0.77 for the stronger half, and LIME's top word matches SHAP's in 86\% against 95\%.

Finally, we asked whether any readily available signal warns an auditor that SHAP is wrong. For the 25\% of identity sentences with the largest error $|\Delta_t - \hat{\Delta}_t|$, weak signal strength flags them with an AUC of 0.80 and low LIME $R^2$ with 0.64, whereas SHAP--LIME disagreement reaches only 0.46, no better than chance.

\subsection{Sensitivity to the Removal Baseline}\label{sec:res-baseline}
% Sources: exp3_baselines.csv, exp3_baseline_rankings.csv.
% Unbiased control-word |phi| rises slightly instead (0.43, 0.47, 0.49); cut for space.
By default, SHAP replaces a removed word with ``...''. Deleting the word instead or using the model's mask token, barely changes the ranking of identity terms ($\rho \geq 0.97$ between every pair of baselines) but does change the magnitudes: SHAP's error against the counterfactual effect falls from 0.94 (dots) to 0.84 (delete) to 0.70 log-odds (mask) for \texttt{original}, and from 0.72 to 0.58 to 0.44 for \texttt{unbiased}.
