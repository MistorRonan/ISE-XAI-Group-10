## TL;DR

Interpretable-NLP work uses different datasets and metrics, which makes progress hard to compare. ERASER fixes this with a benchmark of seven NLP datasets with human-marked **rationales** (the snippets that support a label), plus a standard set of metrics. Some metrics measure **plausibility** (agreement with human rationales). Others measure **faithfulness**: whether the model actually relied on those snippets. The faithfulness metrics are **comprehensiveness** (remove the rationale; confidence should drop) and **sufficiency** (keep only the rationale; confidence should stay). Both are averaged over several cut-off sizes as an **AOPC**, and compared against a **random** baseline. For us, this paper is the definition of our Exp 5 metrics and the reason we include a random baseline. Its own results also contain **negative sufficiency values**, so ours aren't an anomaly.

## Key takeaways

- **The benchmark (§3, Table 1):** seven datasets repurposed from existing corpora: Evidence Inference, BoolQ, Movie Reviews, FEVER, MultiRC, CoS-E and e-SNLI. Each has labels plus human rationales. Some test sets also have *comprehensive* rationales (all supporting evidence marked). Human agreement is reported with Cohen's κ, from 0.618 (BoolQ) to 0.854 (FEVER) (Table 2).
- **Plausible vs. faithful (§2, §4.2):** agreement with human rationales measures plausibility, "but such approaches do not establish whether the model actually relied on these particular rationales". Rationales "that correspond to the inputs most relied upon" are **faithful**. "How best to measure rationale faithfulness is an open question."
- **Agreement metrics (§4.1):** for discrete rationales, token-level IOU F1 (a match if overlap > 0.5) and token precision/recall/F1. For soft scores, AUPRC against human rationales.
- **Comprehensiveness (§4.2, Eq. 1):** comprehensiveness = m(xᵢ)ⱼ − m(xᵢ\rᵢ)ⱼ, the predicted-class probability on the full input minus that with the rationale removed (a "contrast example", following Zaidan et al. 2007).
  - High = the rationale mattered.
  - "A negative value here means that the model became *more* confident in its prediction after the rationales were removed; this would seem counter-intuitive if the rationales were indeed the reason for its prediction."
- **Sufficiency (§4.2, Eq. 2):** sufficiency = m(xᵢ)ⱼ − m(rᵢ)ⱼ, full input minus rationale-only. **Lower is better.** The model "should be able to come to a similar disposition … using *only* the rationales" (Fig. 2).
  - Same sign convention as our Exp 5 (`suff = base − kept`): a negative value means the rationale alone scores *higher* than the full input.
- **From soft scores to rationales (§4.2):** for token-scoring methods like LIME, take the top-k_d tokens, where k_d is the *average human rationale length* for that dataset.
- **AOPC (§4.2, Eq. 3):** to avoid choosing a single k, average comprehensiveness (or sufficiency) over bins of the top **1%, 5%, 10%, 20% and 50%** of tokens. This is adapted from Samek et al. (2016) for image saliency maps.
  - Footnote 7: "similar in concept to ROAR (Hooker et al., 2019) except that we re-use an existing model as opposed to retraining for each fraction". So the model is evaluated on deleted-token inputs it never trained on, which is exactly the OOD issue Hase et al. raise.
  - **Random baseline:** "As a point of reference, we also report these when *random* scores are assigned to tokens", averaged over 10 runs (Table 4).
- **Baselines (§5–6, Tables 3–4):** hard-selection models (Lei et al. 2016, pipelines, BERT-to-BERT) and soft-scoring models (BERT/GloVe + LSTM, explained with attention, gradients and LIME).
  - "Both simple gradient and LIME-based scoring yield more comprehensive rationales than attention weights". Attention does better on AUPRC (plausibility), "in line with prior findings that it may provide plausible, but not faithful, explanation". **"LIME does particularly well across these tasks in terms of faithfulness."**
  - E.g. e-SNLI comprehensiveness: LIME 0.437 vs. attention 0.105 and random 0.081. MultiRC: LIME 0.213 vs. random 0.029.
  - **Negative sufficiency appears in their own results** (Table 4), e.g. LIME −0.128 (Evidence Inference), −0.154 (BoolQ), −0.079 (MultiRC). The paper doesn't comment on it.
  - Random removal is "particularly damaging" for models with high sufficiency scores, "indicating poor absolute ranking".
- **Main argument (§1, §7):** a shared benchmark with standard metrics will help interpretable NLP progress, but the metrics are "a reasonable start" and measuring faithfulness is "a topic ripe for additional research". The leaderboard is "better viewed as a 'results board'; we do not privilege any one metric."

## Why it matters for our project

1. **It's the definition of our Exp 5 metrics (Method III-E).** Comprehensiveness, sufficiency, AOPC and the random baseline all come from here. Method should cite `deyoung2020eraser` when defining them, and say how we differ (see Concerns).
2. **It means our negative sufficiency isn't a bug (Results IV-C, Discussion V).** ERASER's own LIME results have negative sufficiency on three datasets. With Hase et al. (2021) giving the mechanism (OOD inputs make it easy to push confidence to an extreme), we can present our negative values as a known property of the metric, not an error in our code.
3. **It backs our LIME vs. random comparison.** ERASER finds LIME the most faithful soft-scoring method, well above random. Our Exp 5 is similar: LIME 3.05 vs. random 0.35 (comprehensiveness, original). So our deletion results match the benchmark's.
4. **Plausible ≠ faithful supports "agreement ≠ correctness" (Discussion V).** ERASER separates agreement with *humans* from reliance by the *model*. Our version is about agreement between *two explainers* (SHAP–LIME 78/88), but the logic is the same: agreeing with something isn't evidence the explanation reflects what the model used. We use Krishna 2022 for the main point. This is support for the same idea.
5. **It frames the deletion test as one test among several (Discussion).** The authors don't treat these metrics as settled. That supports our design choice of checking explainers against independent references (Exp 3 counterfactual, Exp 4 exact Shapley) rather than trusting deletion scores alone.

## Concerns / why we should be careful with it

- **Our Exp 5 doesn't follow ERASER exactly.** We compute scores in **log-odds**, not predicted-class probability. We use **k = 1–3 words**, not 1/5/10/20/50% bins or k_d. And we always explain the toxic class, not necessarily the predicted class. These are reasonable changes for short template sentences, but Method should say "adapted from ERASER", and our numbers can't be compared with theirs.
- **The metrics assume deletion is harmless.** Footnote 7 says they reuse the trained model on deleted-token inputs without retraining. That is exactly what Hase et al. (2021) show makes the inputs OOD and the metrics socially misaligned. Cite them together.
- **Different tasks and models:** NLI, QA, fact-checking and sentiment with BERT/GloVe + LSTM, not toxicity classification or Detoxify.
- **No significance tests or confidence intervals** in Table 4 (random is averaged over 10 runs, but no spread is given). Small differences shouldn't be over-read.
- **They define the metrics but don't validate them.** The paper proposes comprehensiveness and sufficiency as plausible measures of faithfulness without testing them against a ground truth. So "high comprehensiveness = faithful" is an assumption, which is part of why our Exp 3/4 references matter.

## Where this ties back into XAI module content

- **LIME (week 4, W4-L02):** ERASER evaluates LIME as a soft-scoring explainer and finds it the most faithful of its baselines. Its top-k cut-off is the same thing we do when turning LIME weights into a "top word".
- **Faithfulness vs. plausibility (Jacovi & Goldberg 2020):** ERASER cites them as urging "the community to evaluate faithfulness on a continuous scale of acceptability". That is the `jacovi2020faithfully` entry in our refs.bib, useful for Background II-C.
- **Choosing a baseline (textbook p48):** "removing" rationale tokens is a baseline choice. ERASER removes them outright, and our Exp 3 baseline check shows this choice moves magnitudes.
- **Doshi-Velez & Kim (2017, course reading):** ERASER cites it for the breadth of what "interpretability" can mean, and also for human-judgement evaluation.
