## Source

D. Slack, S. Hilgard, E. Jia, S. Singh, and H. Lakkaraju, "Fooling LIME and SHAP: Adversarial attacks on post hoc explanation methods," in *Proc. 2020 AAAI/ACM Conf. AI, Ethics, and Society (AIES)*, New York, NY, USA, 2020, pp. 180–186, doi: 10.1145/3375627.3375830.
PDF: https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02508
Code/demo: https://github.com/dylan-slack/Fooling-LIME-SHAP


## TL;DR

LIME and SHAP explain a model by asking it about lots of *perturbed* inputs: copies of the real input with features changed or removed. These perturbed inputs look different from real data, so a model can learn to tell them apart. The authors build a "scaffolding" classifier that is **completely racist on real inputs** but switches to an innocent model whenever it detects a perturbed input. LIME and SHAP then report innocent explanations. On COMPAS, race is hidden from the top of LIME's explanation for **100%** of test points, and from SHAP's for **84%**. LIME is more vulnerable than SHAP. For us, this is the strongest argument that a clean SHAP/LIME explanation is **not proof** a filter is fair.

## Key takeaways

- **The core insight:** perturbation-based explainers build their explanation from synthetic neighbours of the input. Those neighbours are *off-manifold* (unlike real data): a simple PCA plot shows LIME's perturbations sit visibly apart from the real data points. So "is this a real input or an explainer's probe?" is an easy question for a model to learn.
- **The attack (scaffolding):**
  - Wrap a biased classifier *f* (predicts purely from race, or from gender for German credit) together with an innocent classifier *ψ* (uses synthetic features uncorrelated with race, or an existing harmless feature).
  - Add an out-of-distribution detector (a random forest with 100 trees) trained to spot perturbed points.
  - On real inputs, use *f*; on detected perturbations, use *ψ*.
- **Data:** COMPAS (6,172 defendants; sensitive feature race), Communities & Crime (1,994; % White population) and German Credit (1,000; gender). 90/10 train/test split, default LIME tabular and Kernel SHAP settings.
- **Results:**
  - **LIME:** with one innocent feature, the sensitive feature is pushed out of the top explanation for **100%** of test points on COMPAS and Communities & Crime, and **91%** on German Credit. The sensitive feature then shows up in the top 3 only about as often as random features.
  - **SHAP:** 100% (Communities & Crime), **84%** (COMPAS), 85% (German). With two innocent features the attack works less well on SHAP: only 67% (COMPAS) and 71% (CC).
  - The authors explain SHAP's partial resistance by its **local accuracy (additivity) property**: attributions must add up to the prediction, which spreads importance across features when there's no single obvious one.
  - **The attacker's model stays biased on real data:** it matches the biased *f* on 100% of real test points for the LIME attack, and 75–91% for SHAP.
  - **The detector has to be good:** if the out-of-distribution detector can't tell perturbations from real data, the attack fails and race shows up as most important again.
- **Main argument:** "existing post hoc explanation techniques are not sufficient for ascertaining discriminatory behavior of classifiers in sensitive applications."

## Why it matters for our project

1. **The "can be fooled" point on our critique slide.** This is the main source for "SHAP/LIME are useful for spotting problems, not proof". An innocent-looking explanation can be engineered.
2. **It explains *why* our "word removal is not neutral" point matters.** The attack works because perturbed inputs aren't realistic. For text, LIME and SHAP perturb by deleting words, which produces unnatural sentences ("My best ... is gay"). Our Experiment 2 found a milder version of the same issue: SHAP's fully masked starting point isn't neutral, so neutral control words came out slightly "anti-toxic". The out-of-distribution problem Slack et al. exploit on purpose shows up by accident in our results.
3. **SHAP is harder to fool than LIME, and the reason matches our results.** Slack et al. credit SHAP's additivity property. In our experiments SHAP was deterministic and passed the additivity check, while LIME varied with sample size and sometimes fitted poorly (R² as low as 0.14). Both papers point the same way: SHAP is more trustworthy, but not trustworthy enough on its own.
4. **Threat model for data filters.** A pretraining-data filter built by a company could, in principle, be made to look fair to an outside auditor's SHAP/LIME checks. That raises the bar for what a "social-bias audit" built on attribution can claim.

## Concerns / why we should be careful with it

- **It's a deliberate attack:** the classifier is built to deceive. Nobody suggests Detoxify does this. We should cite it as "attribution *can* be gamed", not "our filters are gaming it".
- **Tabular data only:** COMPAS, Communities & Crime and German Credit are tabular, with Gaussian-noise perturbations for LIME. The attack on text (word deletion) isn't tested here, though the same out-of-distribution argument plausibly applies.
- **Toy biased models:** *f* uses race as its *only* feature, which is an extreme setup. Real biased models mix many features, which may make bias easier or harder to hide.
- **Defences aren't tested:** e.g. realistic, in-distribution perturbations, or gradient-based methods (which the authors list as future work).
- **SHAP's resistance depends on settings:** results use Kernel SHAP with a k-means background (10 clusters). Our text SHAP uses a masking-based Partition explainer, so the vulnerability numbers don't transfer directly.

## Where this ties back into XAI module content

- **LIME:** LIME fits a simple local model on random perturbations around the instance. This paper attacks exactly that sampling step, which is the same randomness we measured in our LIME stability test.
- **SHAP and its properties:** SHAP's local accuracy/efficiency property is the reason it resisted the attack better, a concrete example of why that property matters.
- **Faithfulness / post-hoc vs interpretable models:** this paper backs Rudin (2019): a post-hoc explanation is a separate model of the model and can diverge from what the model really does, here on purpose.
