## Source

S. Gururangan, D. Card, S. K. Dreier, E. K. Gade, L. Z. Wang, Z. Wang, L. Zettlemoyer, and N. A. Smith, "Whose language counts as high quality? Measuring language ideologies in text data selection," in *Proc. EMNLP 2022*, pp. 2562–2580.
PDF: https://aclanthology.org/2022.emnlp-main.165.pdf
Code/demo: https://github.com/kernelmachine/quality-filter

## What the paper does (short version)

- Re-implements the GPT-3 quality filter: a logistic regression on hashed unigram/bigram features. It is trained to separate "high quality" reference text (Wikipedia, Books3, OpenWebText) from random Common Crawl, and reaches 90.4% F1.
- Applies the filter to a new dataset of ~910K US high-school newspaper articles (1,410 schools), linked to school and census demographics.
- **Topic/style findings:** politics (+35 pts) and sport (+25 pts) score much higher than food. First/second-person pronouns lower the score (−5 pts), and longer documents score higher.
- **Demographic findings:** schools that are larger, urban, public, and in wealthier and more educated ZIP codes score higher. Individual effects are small (~1 pt each), and the model explains R² ≈ 0.14.
- **Political correlation:** county 2016 GOP vote share correlates negatively with scores (r = −0.33). Once it is added to the regression, the rural and wealth variables lose significance. The authors treat it as a proxy for the urban/rural divide and leave it out of the main model.
- **Comparison with human notions of quality:** the filter does not track news factuality (COVID disinformation scored 0.93–0.98). It barely tracks TOEFL essay grades (r = 0.12), where the essay prompt matters far more. It disfavours Pulitzer-winning poetry and drama.
- **Main argument:** every quality filter encodes a "language ideology", so there is no neutral, general-purpose corpus.

## Why it matters for our project

1. **Motivation evidence.** This is the source behind our topic summary claim that quality filters favour text from wealthier, more educated sources. It should be cited in the Introduction and Background.
2. **It audits without explaining.** The authors use regression on metadata and topics, not feature attribution, so they show *that* the filter is biased but not *which features* drive individual removal decisions.
3. **A possible test case with ground truth.** The filter is a public linear model, so its coefficients are effectively the "true" explanation. We could check whether SHAP/LIME attributions agree with the coefficients as a faithfulness test.
4. **Critical angle for the presentation/paper.** "Quality" is defined by the reference corpora, so the filter is biased by design. Attribution can tell us which n-grams matter, but not whether the definition of quality was legitimate in the first place. That is a limit on explanation as an auditing tool.

## Concerns / why we should be careful with it

- **"Political bias" claim:** our working title mentions political bias. This paper's political correlation is confounded with rural geography, and the authors themselves downplay it. We should not cite it as direct evidence of political bias in filters without that caveat.
- **Replica, not the real filter:** no claims can be made about GPT-3's actual behaviour.
- **Narrow data:** US-only, English-only, one amateur genre. It may not generalise to the toxicity classifiers (Perspective, Detoxify) that we also discuss.
- **Hashing vectoriser:** hashed features are hard to map back to words. If we use this filter for attribution, we may need to retrain it with a plain vocabulary vectoriser, and justify that change.

## Proposed next steps (for group discussion)

- Decide whether to use the replicated GPT-3 filter as one of our case-study filters (vs. a toxicity classifier only).
- Check whether the released code/demo can be run locally and whether retraining without hashing is feasible.
- Revisit the "political" framing in our title: keep it, soften it to "social", or address the geography confound explicitly.
- Follow up on cited related work: Dodge et al. (2021) on C4 blocklists, Sap et al. (2021) on annotator bias in toxicity detection, and Gao (2021) on quality filtering and downstream performance.
