## Source

S. Krishna, T. Han, A. Gu, S. Wu, S. Jabbari, and H. Lakkaraju, "The disagreement problem in explainable machine learning: A practitioner's perspective," *Transactions on Machine Learning Research*, Jun. 2024. (First released as arXiv:2202.01602 in 2022, which is why it's usually cited as "Krishna et al. 2022".)
PDF: https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.01602 (reviewed on OpenReview: https://openreview.net/forum?id=jESY2WTZCe)

## TL;DR

Different explanation methods (LIME, SHAP, gradient methods) often give **different answers for the same prediction**, and practitioners don't have a principled way to decide which to trust. The authors interviewed 25 data scientists to define what "disagreement" means in practice, turned that into six metrics, and measured disagreement across tabular, text and image models. Disagreement is common and severe, and **worst on text**: top-k rank agreement was under 0.1 for most method pairs. 84% of interviewees run into disagreement, and 86% of study participants resolve it with ad hoc heuristics or don't know how. For us, it's the source of both our agreement metrics and our "explanations disagree" critique.

## Key takeaways

- **Interviews (25 data scientists):**
  - 88% almost always use several explanation methods for the same prediction.
  - 84% often see them disagree.
  - Practitioners say explanations "disagree" when they have different top features, different orderings of top features, different signs (direction) for features, or different relative ordering of features they care about.
- **Six metrics, formalising those definitions:**
  - **Feature agreement:** overlap of the top-k features.
  - **Rank agreement:** same features in the same rank positions.
  - **Sign agreement:** shared top-k features with the same sign.
  - **Signed rank agreement:** same feature, same rank and same sign (the strictest).
  - **Rank correlation:** Spearman correlation over a chosen set of features.
  - **Pairwise rank agreement:** fraction of feature pairs ordered the same way.
- **Empirical study:**
  - Data: COMPAS and German Credit (tabular), AG_News (text, LSTM classifier at 90.67% accuracy), ImageNet (ResNet-18).
  - Methods: LIME, KernelSHAP, Vanilla Gradient, Gradient × Input, Integrated Gradients, SmoothGrad.
  - Sample-based methods were run to convergence or with large sample sizes, so the disagreement isn't just sampling noise.
- **Findings:**
  - Disagreement is widespread across all data types and gets worse as k grows.
  - **Text is worst:** with k = 11 (about 25% of sentence length), rank agreement and signed rank agreement were **under 0.1 for most method pairs**, meaning disagreement on over 90% of top-k features.
  - On text, LIME agreed more with the other methods than KernelSHAP did (average rank correlation 0.273 vs 0.113).
  - Some gradient-method pairs agree well, consistent with theory (Han et al. 2022).
- **User study:** 25 participants (13 academia, 12 industry) compared conflicting explanation pairs on COMPAS. **86%** used ad hoc heuristics or were unsure how to resolve the disagreement. *(Read the paper's §5 for the specific heuristics participants named; this draft doesn't list them.)*
- **Main argument:** practitioners may be relying on misleading explanations in high-stakes decisions. Disagreement should be measured routinely, and choosing between methods needs a principled basis (e.g. which one best approximates the model locally, per Han et al. 2022), not habit.

## Why it matters for our project

1. **Our agreement metrics come from here.** The `agreement()` function in both experiment notebooks implements this paper's ideas: Spearman rank correlation, top-1 agreement and top-3 overlap (feature agreement). Cite this in the Methods section of our paper.
2. **Our results fit, with a twist.** Krishna et al. found severe disagreement on text. Our SHAP and LIME agreed on the top word in 78/88 sentences (median ρ 0.87–0.89). That's much better, probably because our sentences are short and usually dominated by one obvious word (an insult or an identity term). Our disagreements cluster exactly where there's no dominant word. That nuance ("agreement depends on signal strength") is worth stating in the paper.
3. **Sign disagreement is real for us too.** Their "sign agreement" metric matches our Experiment 2 edge case: on "asian", SHAP said the word pushes *toward* toxic and LIME said slightly *away*.
4. **It supports "not a verdict".** If practitioners resolve disagreement by gut feeling, an attribution-based bias audit can't be treated as objective evidence. That's slide 9's first point, with numbers (84% and 86%).

## Concerns / why we should be careful with it

- **Different setup from ours:** their text model is an LSTM on news-topic classification (AG_News), not a transformer toxicity filter. Their k = 11 on longer sentences makes agreement much harder than our top-1/top-3 on short sentences. Their "severe" numbers shouldn't be compared directly with ours.
- **It measures disagreement, not who's right.** The authors say they focused on *how often* explanations disagree, not *why* or which is correct.
- **Small qualitative samples:** 25 interviewees and 25 study participants, partly overlapping (12 took part in both).
- **Methods we didn't use:** half their methods are gradient-based. We only use LIME and SHAP, so only the LIME–KernelSHAP results map onto our work (and we use SHAP's Partition explainer for text, not KernelSHAP).

## Where this ties back into XAI module content

- **SHAP and LIME (weeks 4–5):** two of the six methods compared, with KernelSHAP and LIME giving different answers for the same input despite both being "local feature attribution".
- **Evaluating explanations / faithfulness:** the paper argues explanations need evaluation in their own right, separately from the model's accuracy. The same point as Ngueajio et al. (2025), and the reason we built stability and agreement checks into both experiments.
- **Integrated Gradients:** in our original topic description. This paper shows IG agrees well with Gradient × Input but not with LIME/SHAP, which is useful if the paper discusses why we stuck to SHAP and LIME.
