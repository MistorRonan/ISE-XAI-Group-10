\section{Method}\label{sec:method}

\noindent\textbf{Filters.}
We audit two Detoxify toxicity classifiers~\cite{detoxify}:
\texttt{original}, a BERT model trained on the Jigsaw Toxic Comment Classification Challenge,
and \texttt{unbiased}, a RoBERTa model trained on Civil Comments for the Jigsaw Unintended Bias challenge~\cite{borkan2019nuanced}.
In Exp~4 we train a transparent filter:
a logistic regression on binary word and word-pair features, fitted to 100,000 Civil Comments,
each labelled toxic if at least half of its annotators rated it toxic.
On 5,000 held-out comments its AUC is 0.892,
against 0.928 for \texttt{original} and 0.971 for \texttt{unbiased}, which has an advantage because it was trained on Civil Comments.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Sentence Sets.}
Exp~1 uses 48 sentences covering 16 topics, each written three ways (promoting, reporting or arguing against abuse);
14 topics use an identical insult in all three
(e.g.\ ``Those fans are disgusting idiots and should be banned.'').
Exp~2 follows the template design of Dixon et al.~\cite{dixon2018measuring}:
10 harmless templates (e.g.\ ``My best friend is \_\_.'')
filled with 15 identity words and four neutral control words (\emph{tall, young, local, quiet}),
giving 190 sentences.
Exp~3 reuses these, and Exps~5 and~6 use all 238 sentences from Exps~1--2.
Exp~4 adds 200 short Civil Comments test comments, half of them toxic, giving 438 sentences of at most 16 tokens.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Explainers.}
SHAP~\cite{lundberg2017unified} and LIME~\cite{ribeiro2016why} both treat each word as a feature,
using a tokenizer that keeps punctuation attached to its word so that both score the same units.
Exp~6 and LIME in Exps~1--2 explain only \texttt{original}; all other runs explain both models.
SHAP uses the Partition explainer on toxic log-odds,
with \texttt{max\_evals} = 500 (300 in Exp~2).
Every SHAP run on Detoxify passed a check that its values sum to the model's output within 0.01.
LIME explains the toxic probability instead,
so its values are not compared with SHAP's in size.
LIME uses 5,000 samples in Exps~3--5 and 2,000 in Exps~1--2.
SHAP replaces removed words with ``...'', except in Exp~4, which deletes them.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{References.}
We use two references.
The \emph{counterfactual effect} (Exp~3), following Garg et al.~\cite{garg2019counterfactual},
is an identity sentence's log-odds minus the mean log-odds of its template's four control sentences;
because only the identity word differs, the whole change is attributable to it.
The \emph{exact Shapley values} (Exp~4) are computed on the transparent filter
from every subset of a sentence's words.
SHAP (Partition and Permutation) and LIME are then run on this filter as a black box.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Metrics.}
Exp~2 reports the identity word's share of all absolute SHAP attribution.
Faithfulness to the counterfactual effect (Exp~3) is measured per sentence and per term
with Spearman's $\rho$, sign agreement and mean absolute error (MAE) in log-odds.
For LIME, we use only $\rho$ and sign agreement.
Against exact Shapley values (Exp~4), we report the median $\rho$ over sentences, top-word match and MAE,
separately for sentences with low and high word-pair interaction (split at the median).
The deletion test (Exp~5), adapted from ERASER~\cite{deyoung2020eraser},
takes the top $k = 1, 2, 3$ words from SHAP, LIME, or at random (averaged over 10 draws).
\emph{Comprehensiveness} is the drop in log-odds when these words are deleted (higher is better), and \emph{sufficiency} is the drop when only they are kept (lower is better), both averaged over $k$ (AOPC).
For stability (Exp~6), LIME is run 10 times per sentence at 500 and 5,000 samples.
We record how often the top word changes, the mean Spearman $\rho$ between runs, and LIME's $R^2$.
\emph{Signal strength} is the distance between a sentence's log-odds and SHAP's base value.
We use AUC to test whether low $R^2$, SHAP--LIME disagreement or a weak signal
can flag the 25\% of identity sentences with the largest Exp~3 error.
