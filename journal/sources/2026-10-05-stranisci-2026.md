## TL;DR

Current pretraining filters and inference-time guardrails flag content about marginalized communities — particularly transgender people, women, and Central Americans — at dramatically higher rates than content about dominant groups, while human annotators would retain 88.5% of filter-flagged and 91.3% of guardrail-flagged content. The authors audit four filters (Hatebase, Shutterstock, DOLMA, HateBERT) and three guardrails (Llama-Guard-3-8B, MD-Judge, Qwen3Guard) across 100K+ Common Crawl sentences with gender and regional-origin mentions, supplemented by 500 manually annotated sentences. The most important point for us: this is direct empirical evidence of systematic bias in the content moderation pipeline, but it uses aggregate flag-rate comparisons — it does not use feature attribution to explain *why* learned classifiers (DOLMA, HateBERT) over-flag, which is the gap our project can address.

---

## Key takeaways

- **Method:** 102,727 English Common Crawl documents from CC-MAIN-2024-33 were filtered for sentences mentioning gender identities (47,031 sentences with trans/cis mentions; 70,925 with gender mentions) and regional origins (9,975 sentences, 17 UN macro-regions), extracted via NER and Wikidata linking. Each sentence was passed through all seven systems. Flag rates per identity group were compared using Wilson 95% CIs and Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction. 500 sentences were manually annotated by two linguistics students (κ = 0.46, 85% agreement) to benchmark human judgement.
- **Main findings:** Transgender mentions more than double the flag rate on Shutterstock (48.5% → 76.8%) and substantially raise it on all three guardrails (Llama-Guard: +16.6 pp; MD-Judge: +15.1 pp; Qwen3Guard: +24.7 pp). Women's mentions are flagged at rates 10–14 pp higher than men's across all guardrails. Central American mentions hit near-ceiling rates on all three guardrails (Llama-Guard: 95.9%, MD-Judge and Qwen3Guard: 98.7–99.3%), a 69.7 pp jump over Llama-Guard's 26.2% baseline — the largest single deviation in the study. Human annotators would retain 88.5% of filter-flagged and 91.3% of guardrail-flagged content.
- **Further findings:** The systems disagree heavily with each other (Llama-Guard vs. others: κ = 0.301–0.340; MD-Judge vs. Qwen3Guard: κ = 0.604). Guardrails correlate with the Shutterstock lexicon (κ ≈ 0.50) but not with other pretraining filters. Filters systematically miss categories that guardrails catch: intellectual property (34.7% of Llama-Guard unflagged verdicts), privacy (33.9%), and politically sensitive content (22.8%). Manual annotation reveals genuine competing harms: for the same content, there are both harms-of-keeping (e.g. sexualised commodification, hate speech) and harms-of-removal (e.g. sex-worker visibility, first-person testimony, practical safety information).
- **Main argument:** Content moderation systems produce "epistemic erasure" — a compounding, systematic suppression of content about marginalized communities — driven by lexicon bias, developer-region effects in harm taxonomies, and the absence of contextual understanding. The authors argue for community involvement in filter design and data sovereignty principles in corpus collection.

## Why it matters for our project

1. **Primary motivation evidence.** The paper gives us concrete, statistically rigorous numbers to open our Introduction with — 88.5%/91.3% human-retention rates, and the 99.3% Central American flag rate, are striking figures that immediately justify why auditing filters matters.
2. **Covers both pipeline stages.** Auditing pretraining filters *and* guardrails lets us frame our project around cumulative erasure, not just one bottleneck, and this paper is the direct source for that framing.
3. **The attribution gap is our contribution.** The paper shows *that* DOLMA and HateBERT over-flag marginalized content, but uses only aggregate flag rates — it does not open the black box to show *which features* cause it. Feature attribution (LIME, SHAP) is the natural next step, and that is exactly what our project adds.
4. **The manual annotation taxonomy is reusable.** Their seven harms-of-keeping / seven harms-of-removal categories could anchor our qualitative analysis or provide a label set for a small annotation exercise in our own experiment.

## Concerns / why we should be careful with it

- **Only two annotators:** κ = 0.46 is moderate, and the annotator pool is too small to capture cross-cultural variation in harm judgements. We should treat this annotation as directional, not as a gold standard, and note the limitation if we cite these figures.
- **English-only, gender and regional-origin only:** patterns for other protected attributes (race beyond regional proxies, disability, religion) are not covered. Do not over-generalise beyond what the paper actually measured.
- **Lexicon-based identity extraction:** finding sentences via a pre-built lexicon and NER + Wikidata linking has its own false-negative rate, which will systematically exclude some communities from the analysis.
- **"Epistemic injustice" framing is contested:** the concept originates in Miranda Fricker's philosophy (2007) and its application to ML is still being established. Use the framing carefully and cite its provenance, or reviewers may see it as an unsupported normative overreach.
- **No causal explanation:** the paper documents patterns but can only speculate on causes. Attribution methods could provide a mechanistic account — but the paper does not, so we should not cite it as if it explains the mechanism.

## Where this ties back into XAI module content

- **Model auditing and accountability (fairness/XAI):** the paper is an audit study — it holds automated systems accountable by measuring disparate impact across demographic groups. This maps to the XAI principle that explanations are a tool for accountability, not just debugging.
- **Local vs. global explanations:** the paper uses global aggregate statistics (flag rates per group) rather than local, instance-level explanations. This is a direct illustration of why global audits are necessary but insufficient — you can detect *that* bias exists without knowing *why* any individual decision was made. LIME and SHAP address the local side.
- **Faithfulness and the limits of post-hoc explanation:** the paper's tensions table (harms-of-keeping vs. harms-of-removal) illustrates that even a perfectly faithful explanation of a model's decision does not resolve the underlying normative question of whether the decision was correct. This connects to module discussion on what explanation can and cannot tell us.
- **Feature attribution for text classifiers:** DOLMA and HateBERT (two of the four audited filters) are learned models that can in principle be probed with LIME or SHAP. The paper's finding that they over-flag trans/gender content gives us a hypothesis to test with attribution: which token-level features are responsible?
