## Source

D. Borkan, L. Dixon, J. Sorensen, N. Thain, and L. Vasserman, "Nuanced metrics for measuring unintended bias with real data for text classification," in *Companion Proc. 2019 World Wide Web Conf. (WWW '19 Companion)*, San Francisco, CA, USA, 2019, pp. 491–500, doi: 10.1145/3308560.3317593.

## TL;DR

The Jigsaw/Perspective team follow up Dixon et al. (2018) by arguing that a single bias number, or one measured at one threshold, hides the *kind* of bias a classifier has. They propose five **threshold-agnostic** metrics that compare an identity subgroup's score distribution with the rest of the data (the "background"): three AUCs (**Subgroup AUC, BPSN AUC, BNSP AUC**) and two **Average Equality Gaps** (Negative and Positive AEG). They also release about **1.8 million real online comments** labelled for toxicity, 450,000 of them also labelled by crowd raters for which identities they mention. Applying the metrics to two versions of Perspective's toxicity model shows the bias-mitigated version improves but doesn't remove the bias: non-toxic comments mentioning identities like "gay" or "homosexual" are still pushed towards toxic. For us, it's the source of the dataset behind Detoxify's `unbiased` model and Exp 4's training data, and its "scores improved but the shift remains" finding is the score-level version of what our SHAP results show.

## Key takeaways

- **Setup (§1):** they assume a test set with reliable labels across groups and use a fairness definition "similar to *equality of odds*" (Hardt et al. 2016). A model has unintended bias if performance "varies across the set of designated groups".
  - **Why threshold-agnostic:** classifiers return *scores* used with many thresholds, so "threshold dependant metrics can obscure the view of unintended bias".
  - **Why compare to a background:** instead of computing metrics on the subgroup alone, each metric compares the subgroup with the rest of the data.
- **The three AUCs (§3.1, Definition 1, Eqs. 1–3).** D⁻/D⁺ = negative/positive examples in the background; D_g⁻/D_g⁺ = negative/positive examples in subgroup g.
  - **Subgroup AUC** = AUC(D_g⁻ + D_g⁺): can the model separate toxic from non-toxic *within* the subgroup? Low = poor understanding of that group.
  - **BPSN AUC** (Background Positive, Subgroup Negative) = AUC(D⁺ + D_g⁻): low when **non-toxic comments about the group score higher than toxic comments in general**, i.e. likely *false positives* for the group.
  - **BNSP AUC** (Background Negative, Subgroup Positive) = AUC(D⁻ + D_g⁺): low when toxic comments about the group score lower than non-toxic comments in general, i.e. likely *false negatives*.
  - Because each AUC takes all its negatives or all its positives from one group, it's **robust to class imbalance** between groups. This is the fix for the problem with Dixon et al.'s pinned AUC, which they analyse in a separate paper (Borkan et al. 2019, "Limitations of Pinned AUC", ref [2]).
- **Average Equality Gaps (§3.2, Definitions 2–4):**
  - The Equality Gap is TPR(subgroup) − TPR(background) at *one* threshold. The **Positive AEG** integrates that gap over every threshold: the area between the curve (TPR_g(t), TPR(t)) and y = x (Fig. 2, Eq. 4).
  - Equivalent form (Def. 3): Positive AEG = ½ − P(Ŷᵢ > Ŷⱼ | i ∈ D⁺, j ∈ D_g⁺). So **positive AEG = the subgroup's positive examples score higher than the background's**. Def. 4 gives a closed form via the Mann–Whitney U statistic.
  - **Negative AEG** is the same with negative examples. Range −0.5 to 0.5; 0 = identical means.
  - **Theorem 3.2:** if equality of opportunity holds at every threshold, AEG = 0. **Theorem 3.3:** AEG = 0 only guarantees equality of opportunity at *some* non-trivial threshold. So AEG = 0 doesn't mean "no bias".
- **What each metric catches (§3.3, Table 2, simulated distributions A–G):**
  - **A, small score shift** (subgroup shifted up but still separable): only the AEGs catch it (0.42/0.42; all AUCs ≈ 1.0).
  - **B, large shift:** BPSN drops to 0.76 and AEGs rise to 0.5.
  - **C, shift plus class skew:** same numbers as B, showing robustness to imbalance.
  - **E, low separability:** Subgroup AUC drops (0.93); the AEG signs show the direction of each shift (−0.48/0.48).
  - **F, wider spread, same mean:** **no metric detects it** (all ≈ 1.0 or ≈ 0). The authors say this may or may not be harmful depending on use.
  - Their summary: the AUCs flag bias big enough to cause mis-orderings (no single threshold works across groups); the AEGs flag shifts even when "perfect thresholding is possible"; "it's always possible that there is subtle bias that the metrics cannot detect".
- **Models (§4.1):** two versions of the Perspective API toxicity classifier: **TOXICITY@1** (shown to be biased around words like "gay" and "transgender") and **TOXICITY@6** (built with the Dixon et al. 2018 mitigation of adding non-toxic examples).
- **Synthetic test set (§4.2–4.3, Table 3):** the Dixon et al. (2018) templates: 77k sentences, 50 identity terms, 50% toxic. Lowest 20 terms shown.
  - TOXICITY@1 **BPSN AUC**: homosexual 0.43, gay 0.57, lesbian 0.67. TOXICITY@6: 0.91, 0.84, 0.98. Big improvement.
  - But the **Negative AEG** barely moves: homosexual 0.50 → 0.45, gay 0.48 → 0.47, lesbian 0.47 → 0.41. Their reading: the upward shift for non-toxic comments with these identities "is reduced but not eliminated".
  - Terms like bisexual, trans, queer and black have high BPSN but high Negative AEG: shifted towards toxic, but not far enough to cause mis-orderings.
  - Subgroup AUC and BNSP AUC are high for nearly all terms in both models, so false negatives are rare.
- **Human-labelled dataset (§4.4, Tables 4–5):**
  - **1,804,875 comments** "sourced from online comment forums", all labelled for toxicity (Very Toxic / Toxic / Hard to Say / Not Toxic, following Perspective's rating guidelines). **450,000** also labelled for identity.
  - Crowd raters picked identities from a fixed list (Table 4): gender, sexual orientation, religion, race/ethnicity, disability. This captures identity content that word matching would miss, e.g. "same-sex marriage" or "people who do not believe in any god".
  - Some identity-labelled comments were **preselected using earlier identity models**, so raters would see identity content often.
  - **Toxicity varies a lot by identity (Table 5):** 8.00% of all comments are toxic, vs male 15.03%, female 13.68%, transgender 21.29%, heterosexual 22.77%, homosexual 28.38%. This is why metrics robust to imbalance matter.
- **Real-data results (§4.5, Tables 6–7):**
  - **Real data shows more bias than synthetic:** lower values and more variation across groups. The weakest groups agree between the two test sets.
  - **Bias skews towards toxicity:** across both models, Subgroup and BPSN AUCs are lower than BNSP, Negative AEGs are positive and Positive AEGs negative. The most-affected groups (black, homosexual_gay_or_lesbian, jewish) are those "frequently attacked in online discussion"; the least affected are male, christian and asian, "again aligned with societal stereotypes".
  - **Mitigation works on short comments (< 100 characters):** homosexual_gay_or_lesbian BPSN 0.57 → 0.82, Negative AEG 0.43 → 0.30. Expected, since the mitigation targeted short comments, where the imbalance was largest.
  - **Much less change on all comments:** same group, BPSN 0.79 → 0.86 but BNSP 0.94 → 0.86, and Negative AEG 0.30 → 0.16. The authors say this "raises the potential that the bias mitigation did not generalize to longer comments".
  - **Groups that got no extra training data didn't improve:** black and asian. On all comments, black's Negative AEG actually went *up* (0.26 → 0.35) and BPSN went down (0.85 → 0.79). *(Read from Table 6; the text only says these groups don't improve.)*
- **Main argument (§5):** use a *suite* of threshold-agnostic metrics, not one number, to diagnose different kinds of bias. Real-data evaluation shows "bias is still present in models that have undergone some bias mitigation".
- **Their stated future work:** choosing thresholds to minimise bias; comparing crowd identity labels with substring matching; a systematic definition of synthetic distributions for testing metrics; "a full taxonomy of different possible biases".

## Why it matters for our project

1. **It's where Detoxify `unbiased` and Exp 4's data come from.** The released dataset became Civil Comments, which Exp 4's transparent filter trains on, and Jigsaw's Unintended Bias competition, which Detoxify's `unbiased` model was trained for. It's the correct citation in Background II-A ("the Jigsaw unintended-bias data") and in Method III-A for Exp 4's data. *(The competition and Detoxify are not in the paper; the link comes from Jigsaw's release and the Detoxify README.)*
2. **Their "reduced but not eliminated" matches our SHAP finding.** On the templates, TOXICITY@6's BPSN AUC for "gay" rose from 0.57 to 0.84, but its Negative AEG stayed at 0.47–0.48: the scores were reordered, but non-toxic "gay" sentences still sit higher than other non-toxic ones. That's the score-level version of our Exp 2 interpretation ("the debiased model lowered the score but the decision still leans on the group word"). Worth citing in Discussion V as independent support, but see the concerns.
3. **It's the "aggregate audit" our gap statement contrasts with.** These metrics say *how* a group's scores are shifted (up or down, mis-ordered or not) across many comments. They can't say *which words* in a comment caused a score. That's exactly the "all of these audit in aggregate; none explains an individual removal" gap in our paper plan (II-A).
4. **Its own limits support our "triage, not certify" verdict.** Scenario F in Table 2 is bias that *none* of their five metrics detect, and Theorem 3.3 shows AEG = 0 doesn't prove equality. Even purpose-built fairness metrics can't certify a model as unbiased, which matches our claim about attribution.
5. **"Mitigated on short comments, not long ones" is a scope warning for us.** Our sentences are all short templates, exactly where Jigsaw's mitigation was aimed and worked best. Our Detoxify results may not describe how it behaves on longer pretraining documents.

## Concerns / why we should be careful with it

- **Different models:** the results are for Perspective API TOXICITY@1 and @6, not Detoxify. Detoxify `unbiased` was trained *on* this data (for the competition), not evaluated in this paper, so none of these numbers describe our models.
- **The competition metric isn't in this paper:** the Kaggle competition scored models with a combination of overall AUC and averages of Subgroup/BPSN/BNSP AUCs. That formula comes from the competition page, not here, so don't cite it to this paper.
- **The labels are assumed reliable:** the whole method rests on "the existence of a test set with reliable labels across a range of groups", and the authors say "the assumption of reliable labels is significant and doesn't hold in all use cases". Toxicity labels themselves can be biased (e.g. Sap et al. 2019 on dialect).
- **Few details on annotation:** the paper doesn't give the number of raters per comment, agreement between raters, how a comment's score is computed from ratings, or the 0.5 cut-off for "toxic" we use in Exp 4. The data source is only "online comment forums", and some identity comments were preselected by models, so the subset isn't a random sample.
- **No statistics:** the tables give point estimates with no confidence intervals or significance tests, and some subgroups are small (e.g. heterosexual, 1,291 comments). Differences of a few hundredths shouldn't be over-read.
- **Notation is ambiguous:** Definition 3 (probability form) and Definition 4 (Mann–Whitney form) only agree under one convention for which sample MWU counts, and v2 already fixed a typo in Eq. 4. If we implement AEG, we should follow Definition 3 and check the sign against Table 3 (positive Negative AEG = subgroup shifted towards toxic).
- **Mitigation results mix two changes:** the paper says TOXICITY@6 "was built using" the Dixon et al. mitigation, but doesn't say what else changed between @1 and @6 (architecture, other training data). So not every improvement can be credited to the mitigation. Same caution as our `original` vs `unbiased` comparison, which also differ in architecture.

## Where this ties back into XAI module content

- **Stakeholders / the Auditor (week 1, W1-L01 p5):** "can I verify this system is non-discriminatory?" This paper is the auditor's toolkit *without* explanations: it measures outcomes by group. Its own blind spots (scenario F, Theorem 3.3) show why the auditor would also want to know *why* a score is high.
- **Global vs local explanations (week 4, W4-L01 p6):** these are global, group-level metrics. Our SHAP work goes the other way: local per-sentence explanations, stacked into group averages.
- **Don't infer causality (Textbook p53):** a high Negative AEG shows the model's scores *co-vary* with identity mentions in real comments. Unlike our one-word template swaps (Exp 3's counterfactual effect), it doesn't isolate the identity word as the cause, since real comments about a group differ in many ways.
- **Model evaluation with AUC (Exp 4 Cell 4):** we already use ROC-AUC to compare the transparent filter with Detoxify. This paper shows that one overall AUC can look fine while subgroup AUCs are poor.
