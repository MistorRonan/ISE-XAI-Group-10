## Source

P. Hase, H. Xie, and M. Bansal, "The out-of-distribution problem in explainability and search methods for feature importance explanations," in *Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems 34 (NeurIPS)*, 2021.
PDF: https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.00786 (v2, 27 Oct 2021)
Code/demo: https://github.com/peterbhase/ExplanationSearch (footnote 1)

## TL;DR

Feature importance (FI) explanations, and the metrics used to evaluate them (Sufficiency, Comprehensiveness), work by *removing* words and seeing how the model's confidence changes. Hase et al. argue that these word-removed inputs are **out-of-distribution (OOD)**: the model never saw anything like them in training, so its prediction on them is shaped by the model prior and random seed rather than by what it learned from data. They call explanations built this way **"socially misaligned"**: they don't communicate what people think they communicate. They compare five ways of "removing" a word, propose retraining the model on randomly masked inputs (Counterfactual Training) to fix the problem, and introduce a search method (Parallel Local Search) that beats LIME and others. The key point for us: **OOD counterfactuals make deletion metrics easier to "win", because it is easier to find word subsets that push the model's confidence to 0 or 1** (§6.3). That is the mechanism behind our negative sufficiency.

## Key takeaways

- **Metrics (§1, §3, Eq. 1):** Suff = f(x)ŷ − f(Replace(x, e))ŷ, keeping only the top features; **lower is better**. Comp is the same formula but removing the top features; **higher is better**. Both are averaged over several sparsity levels (Suff keeps 5–50% of tokens, Comp removes 50–95%), following ERASER (DeYoung et al. 2020, their ref [14]).
  - Same sign convention as our Exp 5: a **negative** Suff means the kept-only-top-words input scores *higher* than the full input.
- **The Replace function defines the counterfactual (§1):** "The only way we can understand what makes some features 'important' … is by reference to a counterfactual input which has its important features replaced with a user-specified (uninformative) feature." So choosing the baseline *is* choosing what the explanation means.
- **The OOD argument (§4, Fig. 1):**
  - Counterfactual inputs are OOD "because they contain feature values not seen in training, like a MASK token for a language model".
  - They reject the older argument (Hooker et al., ROAR) that OOD-ness just confuses "distribution shift" with "removal of information": for one fixed trained model there is no ambiguity about what changed.
  - Their stronger argument: people expect an FI explanation "to reflect how the model *has learned* to interpret features as evidence". On OOD inputs the model has no data to learn from, so its output there is driven by "the model prior and random seed", which people don't expect to influence an explanation. Hence **socially misaligned**, a term they take from Jacovi & Goldberg (their ref [25]).
  - Toy example: a BERT model trained on single sentiment words, explained with a MASK baseline. Every counterfactual equals f(MASK), which the model never saw, so the explanation reflects initialisation, not learning.
  - **"The argument applies equally to explanation metrics"**: Suff/Comp computed on OOD counterfactuals are also socially misaligned.
- **Their fix, Counterfactual Training (CT) (§4):** during training, add a randomly masked copy of each input (sparsity 5–50%) with the same label. Costs only **0.7 points of test accuracy on average** across six datasets.
- **Comparing Replace functions (§5, Fig. 2):** 10 seeds each of BERT-Base and RoBERTa-Base on SNLI and SST-2. Five options are compared: **Attention Mask** (theirs), **Marginalize** (average over BERT-predicted fill-ins), **MASK token**, **Slice Out** (delete the tokens), and **Zero Vector**. Model accuracy under random ablations is used as a proxy for how OOD the inputs are.
  - For standard models, **Attention Mask and MASK token are best**. The best beats the third-best by 1.61–5.48 points. Zero Embedding is up to 10.45 points worse than MASK (BERT, SST-2), and Slice Out is up to 4.72 points worse than Attention Mask (RoBERTa, SST-2). Marginalize is "regularly more than 10 points worse".
  - **CT largely erases the differences** between Replace functions and improves robustness by up to **22.9 points**.
  - Recommendation: CT plus Attention Mask, MASK token or Slice Out. Without CT, use Attention Mask or MASK token.
- **Explanation methods compared (§6, Table 1):** LIME, Integrated Gradients, Anchors and Vanilla Gradients, plus search methods (Random, Exhaustive, Gradient, Taylor, Ordered, and their **Parallel Local Search, PLS**). The setup is six datasets (SNLI, BoolQ, Evidence Inference, FEVER, MultiRC, SST-2), 500 test points each, 10 BERT-Base seeds, and a budget of about 1000 forward/backward passes per explanation.
  - **PLS is the only method that consistently beats Random Search**: up to 12.9 points better than LIME on Suff and up to 20.8 on Comp.
  - **LIME is the best salience method, but Random Search beats it on Sufficiency in 9 of 10 comparisons**, by up to 21.5 points.
  - Integrated Gradients does badly (e.g. SNLI Suff 43.76 vs. LIME 20.00).
- **The finding that matters most for us (§6.3, point 3):** "Suff and Comp scores are often much worse for CT models than for Standard models" (Comp worse on every dataset, by up to 24.16 points). Their explanation: "these metrics are more easily optimized when counterfactuals are OOD because it is easier to identify feature subsets that send the model confidence to 1 or 0." So good-looking deletion scores on a standard model may partly be an OOD effect.
  - **Negative Suff is common in their own results** (Table 1): e.g. PLS on SNLI −1.65, on Evidence Inference −20.76.
- **Main argument (§7–8):** don't create *or* evaluate FI explanations on OOD counterfactuals. Use CT and a sensible Replace function. Explanations "reflect what the model has learned, but it need not be the case that what the model has learned is good".

## Why it matters for our project

1. **It explains our negative sufficiency (Discussion V).** Our Exp 5 Suff is negative (report sentences −3.4): the top words alone score *higher* than the full sentence. Hase et al. give the general mechanism: on OOD word-subset inputs it is easy to find subsets that push confidence to an extreme, and negative Suff shows up in their own tables. Our *specific* story, that the filter sees bare insults without the softening context, is our interpretation and not something Hase et al. test. Phrase it as "consistent with" their argument, not "shown by".
2. **It backs "removing a word isn't neutral" (Discussion V, Results IV-E).** They show the choice of Replace function changes model behaviour by several to over 10 accuracy points, which matches our baseline check (ranking stable, ρ ≥ 0.97, but magnitudes shift: MAE 0.94 → 0.70). Our MAE was **lowest with the mask baseline**, which fits their finding that MASK token is one of the two least-OOD options. That's a nice independent agreement to mention.
3. **It sharpens "triage, not certify".** Their fix (retrain the model on masked inputs) is not available to an auditor of a deployed filter: we can't retrain Detoxify. So an auditor is stuck with OOD counterfactuals, which is one more reason attribution can flag but not certify.
4. **It supports our deletion-test caveat (Results IV-C).** Good comprehensiveness/sufficiency on a standard model isn't clean evidence of faithfulness, since OOD inputs make the metrics easier to optimise. Our Exp 5 should be read alongside Exp 3/4, whose references don't rely on deletion.
5. **Method III-C.** Useful to cite when justifying why we report the removal baseline at all, and why Exp 3 tests several baselines.

## Concerns / why we should be careful with it

- **Different tasks and models:** NLI, QA and fact-checking datasets with BERT/RoBERTa, not toxicity filtering or Detoxify. The OOD argument is general, but none of their numbers describe our models.
- **Their Replace options don't include ours exactly:** our default baseline is `"..."` (dots), which isn't one of their five. "Delete" ≈ Slice Out and "mask" ≈ MASK token. Don't claim they tested our default.
- **Accuracy as an OOD proxy:** §5 measures OOD-ness indirectly, by how much accuracy drops under random ablations. It's reasonable, but it isn't a direct measure of distribution shift.
- **They optimise explanations for the metric:** their methods search directly for the word subset that maximises Suff/Comp, while SHAP/LIME don't. So "Random Search beats LIME" is about those metrics specifically, not general faithfulness. Our Exp 5 has LIME beating random, which doesn't contradict them, because we don't search.
- **Scale differs:** their Suff/Comp are in probability points (×100). Ours are in log-odds, so the magnitudes aren't comparable.

## Where this ties back into XAI module content

- **Choosing a baseline (textbook p48; SHAP, week 4):** "choosing a good baseline can be difficult". Hase et al. turn this into an experiment: the baseline changes model outputs and so changes the explanation.
- **LIME perturbations (week 4, W4-L02):** LIME's local samples are word-removed sentences, i.e. exactly the OOD counterfactuals this paper is about. A low local R² on weak signals (our Exp 6) may partly reflect fitting a surrogate to the model's behaviour on unnatural inputs.
- **Faithfulness vs. plausibility (Jacovi & Goldberg):** "socially misaligned" is a third axis. An explanation can be faithful to what the model does on a masked input yet still not tell you what people think it tells you about the original input.
- **Post-hoc explanations are models of the model (Rudin 2019, week 1):** here the problem isn't the surrogate but the inputs it's built from.
