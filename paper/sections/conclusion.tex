\section{Conclusion and Limitations}\label{sec:conclusion}

We tested whether SHAP and LIME can support identity-term bias audits of a pretraining data filter.
Both rank identity effects faithfully against counterfactual swaps ($\rho \geq 0.92$) and exact Shapley values ($\rho \geq 0.97$), and their top-ranked words matter far more than random ones (comprehensiveness 3.09 vs.\ 0.35).
However, faithfulness is not uniform: SHAP overstates effect sizes, its magnitudes shift with the removal baseline, and LIME's surrogate fits poorly on weak signals.
We therefore recommend that auditors use attribution as a triage tool, to flag suspicious filter behaviour for further review, rather than as a certification of fairness.
In practice: (1)~treat weak signal strength or low LIME $R^2$ as a warning that an explanation may be wrong; (2)~do not treat SHAP--LIME agreement or seed stability as validation; (3)~confirm any attribution finding with a counterfactual swap before drawing conclusions.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Limitations.}
Our scope is purposely narrow: we use short English template sentences with single-word identity substitutions, which do not capture dialect-level bias (e.g.\ AAE~\cite{sap2019risk}) where no single word carries the signal.
We test one filter family (Detoxify \texttt{original} and \texttt{unbiased}), and the two models differ in architecture (BERT vs.\ RoBERTa) as well as debiasing objective, so architectural effects cannot be fully separated.
The transparent filter in Exp~4 is our own construction, not a real pipeline filter.
Our ground-truth references, counterfactual effects and exact Shapley values, exist only because we controlled the inputs; a real auditor facing an opaque commercial filter would not have these.
Future audits should extend to real web text (C4, Common Crawl), the quality filter of~\cite{gururangan2022whose} and human rationale benchmarks such as HateXplain (surveyed in~\cite{ngueajio2025decoding}).
