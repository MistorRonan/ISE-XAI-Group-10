## TL;DR
 
Fake news and hate speech are usually studied separately, even though they feed each other, and the authors argue that explainability is needed in both. They reviewed 26 papers (13 from a database search, 13 from snowballing; 2017–2022) on XAI for detecting fake news and hate speech. They sort the methods into intrinsic and post hoc and summarize the datasets, models and evaluations. The main finding is that post hoc methods (LIME, SHAP and similar) are used for both tasks, while intrinsic methods are mostly used for fake news. Model accuracy was repeatedly found to be a poor guide to how explainable or trustworthy a model is, which matters most for us.
 
---
 
## Key takeaways
 
* **Method:** Literature survey. They searched Scopus and Web of Science with an "explainable/interpretable AND fake news detection OR hate speech detection" query, then snowballed via Google Scholar (Fig. 3, 5). Inclusion required English, peer-reviewed, full text, 2017–2022. The pipeline went from 705 database records to 13 included, plus 13 via snowballing, giving 26. For each paper they extract the objective, XAI method, ML model, datasets, metrics and strengths/weaknesses.
* **Main findings:**
  * Post hoc methods are widely used for both tasks, and intrinsic methods are more common for fake news than for hate speech (Sec. 5.3, Table 1).
  * Few intrinsic hate speech detectors exist, which the authors flag as an open research direction.
  * Performance does not equal explainability: in HateXplain, BERT was the best classifier and bias reducer, but LIME-based explanations scored better on faithfulness, and the best classifier did not do well on the ERASER metrics (Sec. 4.2).
  * Explainability can matter more for trust than accuracy. In Alharbi et al., dEFEND had the best accuracy (0.82) but lower human trust ratings (3.05/5) than TCNN-URG (3.5), while HDSF scored 2.5 (Sec. 3.3).
* **Further findings:**
  * Reis et al. used SHAP on XGBoost over about 172 features, and only 5% of the features appeared in models with average AUC above 0.85 (Sec. 3.1).
  * Kurasinski & Mihailescu found BiLSTM-CNN reached 85% accuracy against 52% for BERT on short texts, but they used accuracy only (Sec. 3.4).
  * Attention-based detectors (GCAN, MVAN) reached about 90% accuracy after 10 retweets, but the authors note skepticism about attention as explanation (Sec. 3.4).
  * In two user studies, explanations improved users' mental models and trust calibration (Mohseni et al.) but also slowed human assessment (XFake, 147 MTurk workers) (Sec. 3.3).
  * Politically biased training data hurt hate speech detection: BiLSTM F1 was 84.8 (neutral), 83.1 (left) and 78.7 (right) (Wich et al., Sec. 4.3).
  * For under-resourced languages (Bengali, Indonesian), XGB and BERT ensembles with LIME, SHAP, LRP and ERASER were used (Sec. 4.4).
* **Main argument:** XAI is essential for trustworthy, ethical and transparent misinformation and hate speech detectors. The two problems are interconnected (Fig. 2 feedback loop) and should be studied together. Detection can be effective without sacrificing transparency.

## Why it matters for our project
 
1. **Method taxonomy.** The intrinsic vs. post hoc split (Fig. 6, Table 1) gives us a framework to justify our choice of explainer, and shows which method families are well-covered versus under-explored.
2. **Accuracy is not explainability.** The repeated finding (HateXplain, Alharbi et al.) supports evaluating explanations separately from classifier performance, not just reporting F1.
3. **Dataset and tool starting points.** The survey names common datasets (FakeNewsNet, LIAR, HateXplain, Davidson, Jigsaw) and the ERASER plausibility/faithfulness metrics we could reuse.
4. **Gaps we could target.** Few intrinsic hate speech models, almost no multimodal work (only one paper, Wich et al. 2021), and little coverage of non-Western languages (Sec. 5.3–5.4).

## Concerns / why we should be careful with it
 
* **Small and dated sample:** Only 26 papers, and the search window ends in 2022, so LLM-based and newer explainers are absent. Don't cite it as a picture of the current state of the art.
* **Selection issues:** The search string's boolean logic (`(explainable OR interpretable) AND fake news detection OR hate speech detection`) is ambiguous, so the hate speech search may not have required an explainability term. Half the included papers came from snowballing, which can bias toward well-cited work.
* **Descriptive, not comparative:** The surveyed studies use different datasets and metrics, so the survey can't say which explainer is best. Many are small or preliminary, and accuracy-only evaluations are common (the authors themselves criticize this).
* **Taxonomy looks loose:** Sec. 5.1 groups LR, SVM, KNN and trees as intrinsic, calls attention models intrinsic in Table 1, and describes model-agnostic methods as embedding explainability "in the model architecture." The terminology is also inconsistent between "extrinsic" and "post hoc."
* **Citation and reporting slips:** Sec. 5.3 attributes the "explainability builds trust" finding to [73] (GCAN), but the trust study is [69] (Alharbi et al.). It also cites [113] and [115] for "performance ≠ explainability," but [115] is ULMFiT. In Sec. 4.3 the figures for [27] seem swapped (0.794 vs. 0.872). Check the original papers before quoting numbers.
* **Regulation is thin:** The conclusion says they analyzed laws and regulations, but the body covers this only in a short closing paragraph of Sec. 5.4.
* **Dataset non-diversity:** The authors admit the datasets are mostly English and Western, so findings may not transfer.

## Where this ties back into XAI module content
 
* **Intrinsic vs. post hoc, model-specific vs. model-agnostic (week X):** This is the paper's central taxonomy (Fig. 6, Table 1).
* **LIME, SHAP, Anchors (week X):** Used across the surveyed papers, e.g. LIME and Anchors as add-ons to BERT/BiLSTM (Szczepański et al.), SHAP on XGBoost (Reis et al.), and LIME, SHAP and Captum compared in Alharbi et al.
* **Local vs. global explanations (week X):** Alharbi et al. use LIME for local and SHAP/Captum for global explanations; Wich et al. compute Shapley values at both component and token level.
* **Attention as explanation (week X):** dEFEND, GCAN, MVAN and Kurasinski & Mihailescu rely on attention weights, with the survey noting skepticism about attention as an explanation.
* **Gradient/propagation-based and perturbation methods (week X):** Layer-wise relevance propagation, sensitivity analysis and partial occlusion appear in DeepHateExplainer and Wang (2018).
* **Surrogates and distillation (week X):** The teacher-student distillation setups in XFake (MIMIC) and Mohseni et al.
* **Evaluating explanations: faithfulness and plausibility (week X):** ERASER's comprehensiveness and sufficiency (faithfulness) and plausibility metrics (Sec. 4.2).
* **Human-centered evaluation, trust and bias (week X):** The user studies (Mohseni, XFake, Alharbi) and the rationale/annotator-bias discussion.
