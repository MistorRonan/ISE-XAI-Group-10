## Source

## TL;DR

The paper (IBM Research) proposes a cheap, scalable pipeline for finding social bias in LLM pretraining data. It first detects mentions of protected groups (e.g. "asian", "muslim", "blind") and then classifies whether each mention is described positively, neutrally or negatively ("regard"). Applied to 3M+ Common Crawl documents, it shows that some groups are described negatively far more often than others. For example, "white" has the highest negative-regard share among race/ethnicity terms, at just over 20%. Downsampling negative-regard sentences sharply reduces stereotyped word associations. For us, it's a second, very different kind of pretraining data filter: it targets *collective* bias rather than individual toxic documents, and its decisions are also unexplained.

## Key takeaways

- **Step 1, protected attribute detection:** 97 keywords across 10 classes (race/ethnicity, nationality, religion, gender/sexuality, disability, economic status, residence, etc.). Keyword matching alone gives false positives ("blind date", "vegan nail polish"). A RoBERTa word-sense-disambiguation model ("Gloss-RoBERTa") filters these out. It was trained on labels from Mixtral-8x22B and agrees with it at Cohen's κ ≈ 0.59, which the authors say is similar to human–human agreement (0.56–0.70).
- **Step 2, regard classification:** a RoBERTa classifier labels each mention Positive / Neutral / Negative. It was trained on 50K Mixtral-8x7B-labelled sentences per keyword and reaches F1 0.91 (micro) / 0.82 (macro), measured against a test set that was also labelled by Mixtral.
- **Bias analysis:** they extend a standard word-co-occurrence score with regard information. Plain co-occurrence mostly surfaces obvious place names, e.g. "arab" → "palestinian". Adding regard surfaces actual stereotypes, e.g. "arab" → "terrorist" (negative) and "generosity" (positive). Checked against the SeeGULL stereotype benchmark, recall@500 rises from 25% to 47% for negative stereotypes.
- **Imbalance in Common Crawl:** negative-regard shares differ a lot by group. "white" (just over 20%) and "black" (around 20%) are highest, and "hispanic" is lowest.
- **Mitigation:** capping every group's negative-regard share at 1% by downsampling reduces stereotyped associations a lot. For example, p("supremacist" | "white") falls to 19% of its original value and p("terrorist" | "arab") to 46%, while positive associations stay the same or rise slightly.
- **Main argument:** social bias is different from toxicity. A sentence like "they were protesting racist white police officers" is harmless on its own, but harmful if over-represented. So it can't be caught by instance-level toxicity filters and should be *rebalanced* rather than deleted.

## Why it matters for our project

1. **A different kind of filter to audit.** Our topic description focuses on toxicity and quality filters. This paper adds a third type: a fairness filter that downsamples by regard. It's a useful contrast case, because it is meant to *reduce* bias, but it still decides which sentences about which groups are removed, and those decisions are unexplained.
2. **Its "explanation" is global and data-level, not model-level.** The word-association tables show what co-occurs with each group in the *data*. They don't show why the regard classifier labelled a given sentence negative. Token-level attribution (SHAP/LIME/IG) on the RoBERTa regard classifier would fill exactly that gap.
3. **Removal can hit the wrong texts.** Their own example of a downsampled sentence is a news report of a racist assault. Combined with Mendu et al.'s "reporting pages" problem, this suggests that both toxicity filters and bias filters struggle with *reporting about* harm. Attribution could show whether the filter keys on the event being reported or on how the group is described.
4. **Ready-made, auditable pieces.** The pipeline is built from small RoBERTa classifiers, which are much cheaper to run attribution on than an LLM. Their prompts and taxonomy are in the appendix, so a similar setup could be reproduced.

## Concerns / why we should be careful with it

- **Political belief is explicitly excluded:** the authors say the taxonomy doesn't cover political belief, age or marital status. It can't be cited as evidence of *political* bias in pretraining data, only social and demographic bias. That matters for our working title.
- **LLM labels all the way down:** both classifiers are distilled from Mixtral, and the regard test set was also checked only by Mixtral. So the F1 of 0.91 measures agreement with Mixtral, not with people. The authors themselves admit Mixtral may be biased.
- **Moderate agreement:** κ ≈ 0.59 for attribute detection is only moderate, even if humans don't do much better.
- **No downstream test:** they never train an LLM on the rebalanced data. They show co-occurrence statistics change, not that the resulting model is less biased.
- **Short paper, limited scope:** English only, Common Crawl only, sentence-level (16–128 tokens), and a fixed keyword list that misses many groups.
- **The 1% cap is a value choice:** they argue a fair dataset should have "balanced regard", but don't justify why 1% or why balance is the right target. Deleting most negative mentions could also remove real reporting about real events.

## Where this ties back into XAI module content

- **Global explanations / dataset-level analysis (week ___):** the frequency + regard bias score is a global, model-agnostic summary of what the data associates with each group. It's a good contrast with local, per-prediction explanations.
- **Post-hoc local explanations: SHAP, LIME, Integrated Gradients (week ___):** the regard classifier is a RoBERTa model making per-sentence decisions that lead to removal, which is a natural target for token-level attribution. For example, does "white" or "racist" drive a negative label, rather than the actual description of the person?
- **Surrogate / distilled models (week ___):** the RoBERTa classifiers are effectively students that copy Mixtral's judgements, which relates to surrogate-model ideas and to the question of whose reasoning an explanation actually reflects.
- **Fairness and bias in ML (week ___):** protected attributes, group-level imbalance and rebalancing by resampling are standard fairness concepts, applied here at pretraining scale.
