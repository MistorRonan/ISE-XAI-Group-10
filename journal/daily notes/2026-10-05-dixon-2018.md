## Source

L. Dixon, J. Li, J. Sorensen, N. Thain, and L. Vasserman, "Measuring and mitigating unintended bias in text classification," in *Proc. 2018 AAAI/ACM Conf. AI, Ethics, and Society (AIES)*, New Orleans, LA, USA, 2018, pp. 67–73, doi: 10.1145/3278721.3278729.
PDF: https://www.aies-conference.com/2018/contents/papers/main/AIES_2018_paper_9.pdf

## TL;DR

Jigsaw's team noticed their toxicity classifier gave harmless sentences like "**I am a gay man**" high toxicity scores. The cause: identity words like "gay" appear far more often in toxic training comments (3% of toxic comments vs 0.5% overall), so the model learned "gay" means toxic. They call this **unintended bias** (specifically *false positive bias*), and measure it with a **synthetic template test set**: the same toxic and non-toxic sentence templates with 50 identity terms swapped in. They also propose new metrics and reduce the bias by adding non-toxic examples containing those terms to the training data. This is the method our Experiment 2 copies, and its "gay" finding is the one we reproduced.

## Key takeaways

- **Definition:** a model has *unintended bias* "if it performs better for comments containing some particular identity terms than for comments containing others". The authors separate this (a property of the model) from *unfairness* (which depends on how the model is used): the same bias is harmful if the model removes comments, but may be harmless if it only sorts them for review.
- **Dataset bias:** comparing how often 51 identity terms appear in toxic comments vs overall in the Wikipedia Talk training data:
  - "gay": 3% of toxic comments vs 0.5% overall.
  - "homosexual": 0.8% vs 0.2%.
  - "queer": 0.3% vs 0.06%.
  - "lesbian": 0.10% vs 0.04%.
  - "muslim": 0.2% vs 0.1%.
- **Template test set:** toxic and non-toxic templates with about 50 identity terms swapped in, giving 77,000 synthetic examples (50% toxic). Bias is measured by grouping results by identity term.
- **Metrics:**
  - **False positive / false negative equality difference:** the sum over terms of how far each term's error rate is from the overall rate (inspired by equality of odds, Hardt et al. 2016).
  - **Pinned AUC:** AUC on a dataset "pinned" to one identity term, mixed with an equal-sized background sample. Its equality difference measures the spread across terms.
- **Mitigation:** add 4,620 assumed-non-toxic Wikipedia *article* sentences containing the over-represented identity terms, to balance the training data. Compare with a random control that adds 4,620 random article sentences, with each model trained 10 times.
- **Results:**
  - Bias-mitigated model: AUC on the phrase templates rose to **0.960**, from 0.952 for the baseline, with no loss on the general test set (0.959 vs 0.960).
  - False positive equality difference fell from **74.13 → 52.94**; the random control got worse (77.72).
  - Pinned AUC equality difference fell from 6.37 → 4.07.
  - False negative variance didn't increase.
- **Their stated future work:** reduce dependence on single words, to handle "biases tied to words used in many different contexts, e.g. white vs black".

## Why it matters for our project

1. **It's our Experiment 2 method.** Our 10 harmless templates × 19 words design is a direct, smaller version of this paper's identity-phrase templates. Cite it in the Methods section as the source of the design.
2. **We reproduced their headline example, years later.** Their motivating case is "I am a gay man" being scored toxic. In our results, "gay" was still the top term on Detoxify's original model (mean P(toxic) 0.45 on harmless sentences vs 0.001 for neutral words). Detoxify is trained on Jigsaw data from the same organisation that wrote this paper.
3. **What we add: *why*, not just *how much*.** Dixon et al. measure bias with scores and error rates, a global, outcome-level audit. They identify the cause ("gay" over-represented in toxic training data) through a separate count of the data. SHAP lets us show, per sentence, that the decision itself rests on the identity word (46% of the decision on average; 69% for "gay"). That's the XAI contribution on top of their method.
4. **Their bias-vs-unfairness split supports our framing.** The same identity-word bias is harmful in a *removal* setting like pretraining-data filtering, which is exactly where we apply it. Worth quoting in the paper.

## Concerns / why we should be careful with it

- **Different model and data:** their classifier is a 2017 CNN trained on Wikipedia Talk comments, not Detoxify (BERT/RoBERTa on Jigsaw Civil Comments). Their numbers don't transfer; only the method and the kind of bias do.
- **Templates are artificial:** synthetic phrases measure sensitivity to identity words, not real-world removal rates. That's the same limitation we list for Experiment 2.
- **Mitigation assumes Wikipedia article sentences are non-toxic:** the added "non-toxic" data isn't labelled. That's reasonable for encyclopaedia text, but an assumption.
- **The fixed word list misses groups and word senses:** the authors note the problem of words like "white"/"black" with several meanings, which affects our results too (e.g. "black" as a colour).
- **Mitigated ≠ fixed:** they say per-term rates are "still not completely uniform". Our finding that Detoxify's "unbiased" model still leans on group words (even more than the original, by SHAP share) is consistent with that.

## Where this ties back into XAI module content

- **Stakeholders / the Auditor (week 1, W1-L01 p5):** a classic audit: does the model treat comments about different groups equally?
- **Global vs local explanations (week 4, W4-L01):** their metrics are global summaries over many examples. Our Experiment 2 adds local SHAP explanations per sentence and then aggregates them (W4-L01 p6: global explanations as stacked local ones).
- **Fairness metrics:** equality of odds, false positive rate gaps and AUC-based subgroup metrics are standard fairness tools. A useful contrast with attribution-based audits, which explain *why* rather than *whether* a model treats groups unequally.
