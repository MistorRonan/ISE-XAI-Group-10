## TL;DR

C4 is a large web-text dataset built from the April 2019 Common Crawl snapshot, used to train T5 and the Switch Transformer, and it was released with very little documentation. The authors document it at three levels: **metadata** (where the text comes from), **included data** (what's in it) and **excluded data** (what the filters removed). They find lots of patent text (some of it machine-translated), benchmark test examples in the training data, and social bias. Most importantly for us, they find that C4's **blocklist filter disproportionately removes text from and about minority groups**: documents mentioning sexual orientations are the most likely to be filtered out, and African American English and Hispanic-aligned English are removed at 42% and 32%, compared with 6.2% for White-aligned English. This is the source behind the "C4's blocklist removes LGBTQ+ and AAE text" claim in our README and topic description.

## Key takeaways

- **The dataset (§2, Table 1):** C4 applies several filters to the April 2019 Common Crawl snapshot. It drops lines that don't end in terminal punctuation or have fewer than three words, drops documents with fewer than five sentences or containing "Lorem ipsum", drops documents containing **any word** from the "List of Dirty, Naughty, Obscene, or Otherwise Bad Words", and keeps only documents `langdetect` classifies as English with probability ≥ 0.99. The authors host three versions:
  - **C4.EN.NOCLEAN** (English, no other filters): 1.1 billion documents, 1.4 trillion tokens.
  - **C4.EN.NOBLOCKLIST** (all filters except the blocklist): 395 million documents, 198 billion tokens.
  - **C4.EN** (all filters): 365 million documents, 156 billion tokens.
  - Comparing the last two isolates the effect of the blocklist alone.
- **Metadata (§3):**
  - The single most represented website is **patents.google.com**; Wikipedia and news sites are also near the top (Fig. 2).
  - C4.EN contains 33,874,654 tokens from US military (`.mil`) sites.
  - From 1,000,000 sampled URLs, 92% were first indexed by the Internet Archive in 2011–2019, but there's a long tail of older text.
  - 51.3% of 175,000 sampled URLs are hosted in the US. India, Pakistan, Nigeria and the Philippines have only 3.4%, 0.06%, 0.03% and 0.1% as many URLs as the US, despite their large English-speaking populations.
- **Included data (§4):**
  - **Machine-generated text:** patents.google.com machine-translates non-English patents into English, and over 10% of the C4 patents come from offices requiring a non-English language (an upper bound on translated documents). Some older patents were also run through OCR.
  - **Benchmark contamination (Table 2):** exact matches of test-set target texts range from 1.87% (TIFU-long) to 24.88% (TIFU-short). Exact matches of GLUE inputs range from 1.8% (QNLI question) to 53.6% (QNLI sentence).
  - **Social bias (§4.3):** UnifiedQA (a fine-tuned T5, pretrained on C4) showed a positive bias towards "Jewish" (67.1% positive) and a negative bias towards "Arab" (37.0%) on 294,000 questions generated with Li et al.'s (2020) script (Table 7). In C4, paragraphs mentioning "Jewish" contained 73.2% positive sentiment tokens vs 65.7% for "Arab" (a 7.5% gap with the SocialSent-derived lexicon). The gap was 4.5% in New York Times articles and absent in Al Jazeera articles.
- **Excluded data (§5), the blocklist:** it was originally made to avoid "bad" words in search-engine autocompletions, and includes words like "porn", "sex" and slurs.
  - **What gets removed (§5.1):** k-means clustering (k = 50) of 100,000 excluded documents found only 16 clusters that are largely sexual (31% of excluded documents). Other clusters cover science, medicine and health, and legal and political documents. The appendix also lists religion, gaming and infant care (A.6).
  - **Which identities (§5.2, Fig. 5):** using PMI between 22 identity-term regexes (Table 6) and being filtered, **mentions of sexual orientation** (lesbian, gay, heterosexual, homosexual, bisexual) have the highest likelihood of removal; "european" and "white" have the lowest. In a manual check of 50 removed documents each, **22% ("lesbian") and 36% ("gay") were non-offensive or non-sexual**, several about same-sex relationships (e.g. marriage, dating).
  - **Whose English (§5.3):** using Blodgett et al.'s (2016) dialect-aware topic model and assigning each document its most likely dialect, the blocklist removes **42% of AAE** and **32% of Hispanic-aligned English** documents, vs **6.2% of WAE** and 7.2% of "other". In the final C4.EN, 97.8% of documents are classed as WAE, 0.07% as AAE and 0.09% as Hispanic-aligned.
- **Main argument:** documentation for web-crawled datasets should cover the metadata, the included data **and the excluded data**. Deciding whether a document is toxic or lewd "goes beyond detecting 'bad' words", because meaning depends on social context and on who is speaking. The authors **recommend against using blocklist filtering** when building datasets from web-crawled data, calling the exclusion a form of **allocational harm** (Barocas et al., 2017).

## Why it matters for our project

1. **It's our core motivating evidence.** This is the paper behind the README's claim that "C4's blocklist removes LGBTQ+ and African American English text", and it's already listed on slide plan 2026-10-06 and in the EXP2 spec. Cite it in the Introduction and Background.
2. **It audits removals in aggregate, which is the gap we target.** The authors measure *which groups* lose text (PMI, removal rates by dialect), across the whole corpus. They don't explain individual removals. This matches our README's point that filters "are audited only in aggregate, and none explains an individual removal".
3. **It supports Experiment 2's design.** Fig. 5 shows that identity words alone (especially "lesbian" and "gay") are linked to removal. Our identity-term swap tests the same kind of sensitivity in a learned filter (Detoxify) instead of a word list.
4. **It supports Experiment 1's framing.** The authors' point that toxicity "goes beyond detecting 'bad' words" and depends on context is the question Experiment 1 asks: does the filter react to the insult word or to the intent?
5. **Why a blocklist doesn't need XAI, but our filter does.** For a blocklist, the reason for a removal is already known: the listed word. Learned classifiers like Detoxify don't come with that reason, which is why post-hoc attribution (SHAP, LIME) is needed. Worth stating in our Background to justify why we study a classifier rather than the C4 blocklist itself. *(This is our reasoning, not a claim the paper makes.)*

## Concerns / why we should be careful with it

- **It's about a blocklist, not our filter:** the removal rates are for C4's word list. They don't tell us how Detoxify behaves, only that filtering in general can fail these groups.
- **The dialect labels are a proxy:** the dialect model was trained on 60M geolocated tweets and uses US census race/ethnicity data as topics; each document gets whichever dialect has the highest probability. The authors deliberately don't infer author identity, and note there is disagreement about the dialect terms. We should say "text classified as AAE", not "text by Black authors".
- **The AAE and Hispanic-aligned groups are tiny in the final corpus** (0.07% and 0.09% of C4.EN). The paper doesn't give the absolute numbers behind the 42% and 32% removal rates.
- **Most removed "lesbian"/"gay" documents in the sample *were* offensive or sexual:** only 22% and 36% of a 50-document sample weren't. The finding is that the blocklist also removes much harmless text, not that most removals were wrong. Don't overstate it.
- **The bias result isn't causal:** the authors say they "have not shown a causal link" between C4's sentiment statistics and UnifiedQA's bias, and the C4 gap depends on the lexicon (7.5% SocialSent, 2.0% VADER, 1.4% the manual lexicon).
- **Scope:** one snapshot (April 2019), English only. The authors note that tools like LangID work better for English and their analyses may not generalise to other languages. They also say they only examined some of the possible issues (e.g. personal data and copyrighted text were left to future work).
- **Small errors in the text:** §6 says the blocklist "is more likely to include minority voices", which contradicts §5 and is presumably meant to be "exclude". §5.1 describes PCA projections of TF-IDF, while the Fig. 6 caption says "tSNE PCA". Quote from §5 and §9 rather than that sentence in §6.

## Where this ties back into XAI module content

- **Stakeholders / the Auditor (week 1, W1-L01 p5):** this paper is a dataset audit asking whether filtering treats text from and about different groups equally, the Auditor's "is this system non-discriminatory?" question.
- **Global vs local explanations (week 4, W4-L01 p6):** the paper's PMI scores and dialect removal rates are global, corpus-level summaries. Our experiments add local per-sentence attributions and aggregate them.
- **Don't infer causality from attributions (Textbook p53):** the paper itself is careful to call its sentiment–bias link correlational. A useful parallel when we write up what SHAP/LIME can and can't show.
